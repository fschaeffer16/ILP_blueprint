How the thesis stacks up against the best states for ESE

Which states actually rate highest for special education, what they do differently, and a frank, sourced scorecard of our "meet them where they are" ESE thesis — written for review before we build.

The headline

You're not fighting best practice. You're describing it — plus one thing nobody measures.

The most important local finding: Florida already has this program's classroom model in statute. "Support facilitation" — an ESE teacher delivering specially designed instruction inside the general-ed room, on the same curriculum — is Florida's own named service-delivery model. In St. Lucie, this program isn't an outside philosophy. It's "run Florida's own model at full strength, with evidence no one has had before."
How "best" is measured

No official ranking exists — four proxies, and they disagree

  1. Federal ratings (U.S. DoE, annual). 20 states earned "Meets Requirements" in the 2026 cycle — Florida among them. Only five states have earned it every year since 2014 (Kansas, Massachusetts, Missouri, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin). Measures compliance more than lived quality.
  2. Inclusion rates — % of students with disabilities in regular class 80%+ of the day. National average ~67–68% and rising. Alabama leads (~83–84%); Vermont tops a 2025 fifty-state index; New Jersey is the most restrictive (~44.6%) with the heaviest separate-school use in the country.
  3. Outcomes — test scores and graduation rates for students with disabilities (graduation ~70.6% nationally and rising; Florida among the fastest improvers).
  4. Third-party indexes — a 2025 index ranks Vermont #1, North Carolina #2, Maine #3.
The proxies conflict — and that's the lesson. Alabama is #1 on inclusion with middling outcomes; New Jersey is the most segregated yet rates "Meets Requirements" with strong graduation numbers. Inclusion, compliance, and outcomes are three different things — which is exactly why a program that honestly measures its own outcomes stands apart.
Florida's position

Better than you'd guess

Meets Requirements — 2026 federal determination.
Top-tier inclusion — roughly top 5–10 nationally: ~75–78% of ESE students in regular class 80%+ of the week.
Support facilitation — the in-room, same-curriculum ESE support model is Florida's own, in statute and course codes.
Funding doesn't reward restrictive placement — Florida's Matrix of Services weights by a student's service intensity, not by placement setting.
What the top states do differently

Five moves, all familiar

  • They operationalize the gen-ed presumption. Alabama built statewide co-teaching demonstration sites; research confirms placement is driven by beliefs and district policy, not student characteristics — so presumption genuinely moves placement.
  • MTSS in general education as the chassis (the SWIFT framework — Vermont, Maryland, New Hampshire, Oregon, Mississippi). The same architecture ILP is built on.
  • In-class support models — co-teaching nationally; support facilitation in Florida.
  • Early identification and preschool inclusion, with gains that persist — backing the program's "as soon as possible" bias.
  • Funding designed not to reward segregation (Vermont's 2018 reform; Florida's intensity-based Matrix). Nuance: funding design alone doesn't produce inclusion — New Jersey proves it.

Recommended adjustments · your call

Four nuances the program should absorb

  1. Supported inclusion, never bare placement. The evidence backs inclusion with the behavior tech, the channel, and the facilitation. Placement alone guarantees nothing — keep the supports attached to every claim.
  2. Mismatch cuts both ways. Broaden "below their intelligence" to "mismatched to the learner — in either direction." Claims both the well-evidenced too-hard case and the too-easy case that ILP makes visible.
  3. Inclusion ≠ outcomes automatically (the Alabama / New Jersey paradox). The program's answer is its honest self-measurement.
  4. Presume competence, assessed. The least-dangerous-assumption decision rule, paired with individualized AAC assessment.
Keep the percentage a hypothesis, keep the supports attached — and this program is defensible in front of any ESE director in the country.
