# How the thesis stacks up: the highest-rated state ESE programs

The sourced benchmark for the ["Meet Them Where They Are" ESE Program](ese-program.md): which
states actually rate highest, what they do differently, and a frank scorecard of our thesis
against the published evidence. Written to be reviewed before any build proceeds.

> **Headline: the thesis is not a rebellion against best practice — it is the direction the
> best-performing states already moved, with one genuinely novel claim on top.** And the single
> most important local finding: **Florida already has the thesis's classroom model in statute.**
> "Support facilitation" — an ESE teacher delivering specially designed instruction inside the
> general-ed room, on the same curriculum — is *Florida's own named service-delivery model*. The
> program doesn't ask St. Lucie to defy the state; it asks them to run Florida's own model at
> full strength, with evidence no one has had before.

---

## 1. There is no official ranking — four proxies, and they disagree

1. **Federal determinations (OSEP/IDEA Part B).** The U.S. DoE rates every state annually. In the
   2026 cycle, 20 states earned "Meets Requirements" — **Florida among them**. Only five states
   have earned it *every* year since 2014: Kansas, Massachusetts, Missouri, Pennsylvania,
   Wisconsin. Caveat: this measures compliance and assessment participation more than lived quality.
2. **Inclusion rates (LRE, IDEA §618 data).** The % of students with disabilities in regular
   class **80%+ of the day** — nationally ~67–68% and rising (a 2026 GAO report found a 25%
   rise in gen-ed placement since 2012-13, but with wide state divergence: *inclusion depends on
   geography*). **Alabama leads the nation (~83–84%)**; **Vermont** tops the 40%+ measure (~96%)
   and a 2025 fifty-state index. **New Jersey is the most restrictive state — only ~44.6%** at
   the 80%+ level, with the heaviest separate-school use in the country.
3. **Outcomes.** NAEP scores and graduation rates for students with disabilities, state by state
   (SWD NAEP fell or stagnated nationally in 2024; graduation ~70.6% and rising, with Florida
   among the fastest improvers).
4. **Third-party indexes.** A 2025 index ranks **Vermont #1, North Carolina #2, Maine #3.**

**The proxies conflict, and that's instructive:** Alabama is #1 on inclusion with middling
outcomes; New Jersey is the most segregated yet "Meets Requirements" with strong SWD graduation.
Inclusion, compliance, and outcomes are three different dimensions — which is exactly why a
program that measures *its own* outcomes honestly stands out.

## 2. Where Florida stands (better than you'd guess)

- **"Meets Requirements"** in the 2026 federal determinations.
- **Top-tier inclusion** — roughly top 5–10 nationally, with ~75–78% of ESE students in regular
  class 80%+ of the week.
- **Support facilitation is Florida's named model** (in statute and course codes, documented by
  Disability Rights Florida and the Florida Inclusion Network): the ESE teacher supports the
  child *inside* the gen-ed room on the same curriculum. This is the program's "pull-aside on
  the same module chain," already institutionalized.
- **Funding does not reward restrictive placement** here: Florida's Matrix of Services weights by
  a student's *service intensity*, not by placement setting — structurally aligned with inclusion.

**Pitch implication:** in St. Lucie, this program is *"help Florida's own model reach the kids it
misses, with evidence"* — not an import of someone else's philosophy.

## 3. What the top states actually do differently

1. **Operationalize the gen-ed presumption.** Alabama built statewide co-teaching/co-planning
   demonstration sites + PBIS through its state improvement plan. Research (Agran et al. 2020)
   confirms placement for students with severe disabilities is driven by *beliefs and district
   policy*, not student characteristics — so presumption genuinely moves placement.
2. **MTSS in general education as the chassis.** The OSEP-funded SWIFT framework (Maryland,
   Mississippi, New Hampshire, Oregon, **Vermont**) treats equity-based MTSS as the driver of
   inclusive education — the same architecture ILP is built on.
3. **In-class support models** — co-teaching nationally; support facilitation in Florida.
4. **Early identification and preschool inclusion**, with research showing gains that persist
   into elementary school and beyond — supporting the program's "as soon as possible" bias.
5. **Funding designed not to reward segregation** (Vermont's Act 173 census-block reform;
   Florida's intensity-based Matrix). Nuance: funding design alone doesn't produce inclusion —
   New Jersey uses census-based funding and is still the most restrictive state.
6. **Investment in staffing and educator preparation** (the backbone of Vermont's #1 ranking).

## 4. The scorecard: our thesis vs. the evidence

| Thesis element | Verdict | Grounding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mainstream early; presume the gen-ed placement | **Matches best practice** | Alabama's inclusion machinery; Vermont/SWIFT; federal early-inclusion policy; preschool-inclusion research |
| Pull-aside support on the *same* curriculum | **Matches — Florida literally names this model** | FL support facilitation; a 2025 meta-analysis shows supported-inclusion interventions produce large effects |
| **AAC channel as behavior prevention** | **The strongest-evidenced plank in the whole program** | Functional Communication Training (Carr & Durand 1985 →) is top-tier evidence; AAC-based FCT meta-analyses show large reductions in challenging behavior — **with larger effects in younger children**, which directly supports "as soon as possible" |
| Nonverbal/semi-verbal kids are the right target group | **Solid** | Meta-analytic risk-marker research: communication impairment is a leading predictor of self-injury and aggression |
| Frustration with mismatched instruction drives behavior | **Well-supported, with one nuance** | The curricular-revision literature shows task mismatch produces escape-maintained behavior and fixing the mismatch reduces it. Nuance: *too-hard* is better evidenced than *too-easy* — the defensible claim is "instruction mismatched to the learner, in either direction" |
| Peer models require inclusion | **Solid** | Peer-mediated intervention meta-analyses (in inclusive settings) improve social communication for children with ASD — and peer models only exist where peers are |
| "A % of behavior problems are acquired — and preventable" | **Credible mechanism; unquantified; genuinely novel** | Preventive FCT studies (Hanley's Preschool Life Skills; Fahmie 2016) show problem behavior *emergence can be prevented* in controlled settings. **No state and no study measures acquired-behavior prevention at scale. Nobody. This is the program's differentiator — provided it stays framed as the hypothesis the pilot tests, never a promised number.** |
| Segregated settings breed behavior (mimicry) | **Suggestive — keep it soft** | Peer-contagion/"deviancy training" research is robust for aggregated at-risk/EBD groups (documented from age 5); it has *not* been specifically demonstrated for self-contained ASD/ID classrooms. State the mechanism as risk, not fact |
| Presume competence | **Right instinct; use the careful version** | Anchor to Donnellan's "least dangerous assumption" (a decision rule: when uncertain, the costlier error is assuming inability) **plus individualized AAC assessment** — and steer clear of claims associated with discredited facilitated communication |

## 5. Four nuances the program must absorb (recommended edits, pending review)

1. **Supported inclusion, never bare placement.** The evidence supports inclusion *with* the
   behavior tech, the channel, and the facilitation — placement alone guarantees nothing (GAO;
   teacher-preparation gap findings). The program already says this; keep it loud.
2. **Mismatch cuts both ways.** Broaden "below their intelligence" to "mismatched to the learner
   — in either direction." The too-easy case is real (and ILP uniquely makes it *visible* via
   mastery data); the too-hard case is the better-evidenced one, and the program should claim both.
3. **Inclusion ≠ outcomes automatically** (the Alabama/New Jersey paradox). The program's answer
   is its honest self-measurement — that's what separates it from inclusion-rate chasing.
4. **Presume competence, assessed.** Pair the least-dangerous-assumption decision rule with
   individualized AAC assessment; never imply every nonverbal child hides grade-level ability.

## 6. Bottom line

The thesis stands on three tiers of ground: its classroom mechanics **match what the
highest-rated states already do** (and match Florida's own named model); its strongest causal
plank — **give the child a channel and challenging behavior drops** — sits on some of the
best-replicated evidence in the behavioral literature; and its novel claim — **a measurable
percentage of behavior problems are acquired and preventable through early supported
inclusion** — is a plausible extrapolation *no state currently measures*, which makes it not a
liability but the program's research contribution. Keep the percentage a hypothesis, keep the
supports attached, and this program is defensible in front of any ESE director in the country.
